A day by the window

Morning light crossed the curtain and left a slowly moving edge on the floor. I moved a glass of water aside and made room for the books I had been meaning to put away. There was no music, only the occasional sound of a car passing in the distance and leaves touching one another outside.

The bookcase stood against the north wall. It had five shelves: maps brought back from journeys on the top, novels and essays below them. Small notes were tucked between pages. Some recorded a date on which a book had been borrowed. Others held half a sentence that I had wanted to remember. Rather than decide on categories at once, I took the books out and laid them where I could see them.

It had started as a search for a dictionary. Within an hour, nearly every book was on the floor. There were old paperbacks with soft corners, large illustrated volumes, and new books still wrapped in their original covers. I put reference books in one group, fiction and essays in another, school books in a third, and the notebooks I could never quite classify in a fourth.

The reference books were fewer than I had expected. They would fit together at eye level, where they were easiest to reach. I arranged the dictionaries from left to right and left a narrow space beside them for a book that was being used. The novels took longer. Alphabetical order seemed sensible until I tried it and found that it separated several books I liked to read together.

I tried a different arrangement. Books I had finished went on the lower shelf, while those I wanted to read next stayed above them. A row did not have to be perfectly uniform to be useful. Some spines were taller and some covers brighter, but the books began to look like a collection rather than a stack waiting to be moved.

The oversized volumes needed their own solution. I laid them flat on the lowest shelf, with the largest underneath. There was room for a small wooden tray and the green plant that had been sitting on my desk. The middle shelf had once been empty. Now I reserved it for books I often opened but could not easily assign to a subject: a cooking notebook, a travel diary, and a book of drawings from a friend.

I put a small bookend at either side. One was made from an old brick wrapped in cloth. The other was a round tin with two unused textbooks inside it for weight. After counting the shelves again, I found that almost nothing had been discarded. The same objects simply occupied their places more comfortably.

On the top shelf I left some air around a clock, a small lamp, and a box of postcards. These were things I could enjoy looking at without reaching for them every day. I wrote a number under the front edge of each shelf and made a short note of what belonged there. Then I wiped the dust away and opened the door to let the room breathe.

By noon, the rectangle of light had passed the edge of the desk. The dictionary was back in an obvious place. A few slim booklets stood beside a bookend, and the things I could not yet decide about went into a shallow paper box. I wrote the year on the lid and drew a small leaf beside it, hoping that the mark would remind me where to look.

The kettle began to make a low sound in the kitchen. I made tea and brought a collection of essays to the table. In it, a writer described a path along a river, with new leaves in spring and fallen leaves in autumn. The passage brought back a walk I had taken years ago: a cool stone railing, quiet water under a bridge, and birds leaving small ripples as they moved.

The street was busier in the afternoon. Someone pushed a bicycle past the building, a bundle of flowers still wrapped in paper in its basket. Across the way, a window opened and closed. Clothes hanging in the sun moved slowly in the wind. I closed the book and decided to walk without choosing a destination.

A repair shop had opened at the corner. An old lamp hung above the door, and radios and metal boxes were visible on a shelf inside. The owner was putting a new cover on a wooden chair. He told a waiting customer that the glue needed time to settle. I paused outside and thought that this was a useful description of the day: some things needed arranging, and others needed a little time.

Beyond the shop was a small park. On one bench an older man had folded a newspaper into four. A child watched ants on the back of a leaf. Wind moved through the grass, and the flag at the far end of the path lifted briefly. By the time I had walked around the park, the tightness in my shoulders had eased.

When I returned, the sky had changed from bright blue to a softer grey. There were still just as many books in the room, but the room no longer felt crowded. On the desk I had kept one pen, the tea cup, and a few notes. I tried joining the ideas in those notes into complete sentences, as though gathering loose stones into one hand.

That evening I turned on the lamp and returned to the page I had been reading. Light fell on the paper. The sounds outside gradually became more distant. A few plans were still unfinished, but they no longer seemed to be waiting all at once. The books had places, and tomorrow's work could begin with one small step.

Reading does not need to keep the same speed. Sometimes a sentence asks for a pause. Sometimes a few pages can pass quickly. A comfortable page lets the eye follow the words naturally and gives the hand a steady sense of movement. At the edge, that movement returns gently to rest.

The last bus of the evening passed the corner. Its lights traced a brief line across the wall and disappeared. I put the pen in a drawer, closed the book, and left a little space for tomorrow's tea. Nothing remarkable had happened, yet the details I had noticed again made the ordinary day feel complete.
